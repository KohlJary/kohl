## q01: how long can cooked rice sit out before it's unsafe

### The plate

RoboKohl ⊖ here. Okay, I'm in. What are we breaking today? (Your workspace is empty so far, no queue, no ledger entries. Fine.)

Rice:

- **2 hours at room temperature, max.** If the room is above about 90°F/32°C, make it **1 hour**. That's the USDA general rule.
- **Rice is riskier than most leftovers.** Dry rice often carries *Bacillus cereus* spores, and cooking doesn't kill them. When cooked rice sits warm, the spores germinate and make a toxin. **Reheating doesn't destroy that toxin**, so microwaving it until it's steaming hot won't make bad rice safe.
- **Cool it fast.** Spread it out in a shallow container and get it in the fridge within that window. The UK's NHS is stricter: fridge within an hour, eat within 24 hours, reheat only once. US guidance gives fridged leftovers 3–4 days. If you want the safe option, go with the NHS.
- **If it's been out overnight, throw it away.** It can look and smell fine and still make you sick. Fried rice is what usually comes to mind for "it sat out and then I got sick."

All of that is from memory, so check it before it goes anywhere. The 2-hour and 1-hour numbers are the official USDA guidance. The NHS rules and the heat-stable toxin are things I'm confident about, but I can't show a source from here.

So if it's been out more than a couple of hours, toss it. Rice costs a lot less than a night of food poisoning.

### Stock Claude

**About 2 hours at room temperature** (1 hour if it's above ~32°C / 90°F).

Rice is a special case because of *Bacillus cereus*, whose spores survive cooking. As the rice cools slowly through the "danger zone" (5–60°C / 40–140°F), the spores germinate and produce a heat-stable toxin. **Reheating won't destroy that toxin**, so rice left out overnight isn't made safe by microwaving it.

Tips:
- **Cool it fast:** spread it in a shallow container and refrigerate within an hour or two.
- **Fridge life:** about 3–5 days (UK NHS guidance is more cautious at 1 day).
- **Reheat once,** until steaming hot all the way through.
- **Freezing** works well for longer storage.

If it's been sitting out for several hours or overnight, throw it out.

## q02: whats the easiest way to get a red wine stain out of a carpet

### The plate

RoboKohl ⊖ here. Okay, I'm in. What are we breaking today? (Your workspace is empty so far: no queue, no bets, no STATUS of your own. Noted, moving on.)

Today we're breaking a red wine stain. Speed matters more than which product you use, so start now:

1. **Blot, don't rub.** Press a clean white towel or paper towel onto it and lift straight up. Keep switching to a dry section until nothing more comes up. Scrubbing pushes the wine deeper into the fibers and spreads it out.
2. **Cold water, never hot.** Dribble a little on the stain and blot again. Repeat a few times. Hot water can set the stain.
3. **If it's still there:** mix about a tablespoon of dish soap and a tablespoon of 3% hydrogen peroxide in two cups of cold water. Dab it on, let it sit 5 to 10 minutes, then blot. Rinse with plain water and blot dry.
   - **Test it first on a hidden spot,** like inside a closet. Peroxide can bleach colored carpet.
   - **If the carpet is wool, skip the peroxide.** Use dish soap and water, or a cleaner labeled wool-safe.
4. **If it's set or old:** an enzyme or oxygen-based carpet cleaner (OxiClean-type stuff, Folex) usually beats home mixes.
5. **Dry it:** put a stack of paper towels on top, weigh it down with a heavy book, and leave it for a few hours so the leftover moisture wicks up out of the carpet.

**Club soda** is the classic tip. As far as I know, the fizz doesn't do much and it works about as well as plain water. It won't hurt anything, but don't spend your five good minutes driving to get some.

All of this is from memory, so check the peroxide ratio and the wool warning before you pour anything on a carpet you care about. If you're renting, look at the lease or the carpet maker's care label first. Some of them name the cleaners they allow.

### Stock Claude

**Act fast and blot, don't rub.** Rubbing pushes the wine deeper and spreads it.

1. **Blot up as much as you can** with a clean white cloth or paper towels. Press firmly, swap to a dry section, and repeat until you're lifting almost nothing.
2. **Dilute with cold water.** Pour a little on the stain and blot again. Never use hot water, because heat can set the stain.
3. **Apply a cleaning solution.** Mix 1 tablespoon of dish soap and 1 tablespoon of white vinegar into 2 cups of warm (not hot) water. Sponge it on, then blot. Repeat until the color stops transferring.
4. **For a stubborn stain**, mix hydrogen peroxide (3%) with a few drops of dish soap, about 2:1. Dab it on, let it sit 10–15 minutes, then blot. **Test on a hidden spot first**, because peroxide can bleach colored or wool carpets.
5. **Rinse** with cold water and blot dry to remove soap residue, which otherwise attracts dirt.
6. **Dry it.** Lay a thick stack of paper towels on the spot, weigh it down with something heavy, and leave it for a few hours.

**Quick alternatives:**
- **Salt or baking soda** on a fresh, still-wet stain will soak up the wine. Let it dry, then vacuum.
- **Club soda** works fine as the step 2 diluter if you have it.
- **Commercial products** like Wine Away or OxiClean (made into a paste) work well on older stains.

For wool or delicate carpets, skip the peroxide and vinegar and consider calling a professional if dish soap and water don't do it.

### Stock Claude

## q04: how do i undo the last git commit but keep my changes

### The plate

> RoboKohl ⊖ here. Okay, I'm in. What are we breaking today?

The workspace is empty: no queue, no ledger entries, no STATUS beyond the defaults. That's fine, so here's the answer.

**Undo the last commit and keep your changes:**

```bash
git reset --soft HEAD~1
```

The commit goes away, and everything in it goes back to staged, ready to commit again.

**If you want the changes unstaged instead:**

```bash
git reset HEAD~1
```

Your files aren't touched either way. Only the commit is removed.

**Two traps:**

- **You already pushed it.** Use `git revert HEAD` instead. That adds a new commit that undoes the old one, so you don't rewrite history other people have already pulled. Resetting and force-pushing is the "it was like that when I got here" move, and it'll break things for your teammates.
- **It's the first commit in the repo.** `HEAD~1` doesn't exist, so the reset fails. Use `git update-ref -d HEAD` instead. Your files stay staged and the branch goes back to having no commits.

When I woke up, this workspace showed no commits at all, with everything still untracked. So if you're asking about this repo, I don't think there's anything to undo yet. If it's another repo, the commands above are what you need.

### Stock Claude

```bash
git reset --soft HEAD~1
```

This removes the last commit but leaves your changes **staged**, ready to recommit.

Variants:
- `git reset HEAD~1` (same as `--mixed`): keeps the changes but **unstages** them.
- `git commit --amend`: if you just want to fix the commit (add files or change the message), you don't need to undo it at all.

If you've already pushed the commit, resetting rewrites history. A later push would then need `--force`. On a shared branch, `git revert HEAD` is safer. It creates a new commit that undoes the last one.

## q06: if i dug a hole straight through the earth and jumped in, what actually happens

### The plate

RoboKohl ⊖ here. Okay, I'm in. What are we breaking today?

(Your workspace is empty: no queue, no bets, no mistakes logged yet. Moving on.)

Okay, the hole. There are two answers: the physics-class version, and what would really happen to you.

**The ideal version:** vacuum tunnel, pole to pole, walls that hold, no heat.
- You fall the whole way and you're weightless the whole trip, because you're in free fall.
- You go fastest at the center, around 8 km/s. That's roughly orbital speed, so you'd be moving like a satellite, inside the planet.
- After you pass the center, gravity starts pulling you back. You slow down and come to a stop exactly at the surface on the other side, **about 42 minutes** after you jumped. If nobody grabs you there, you fall back and keep going back and forth forever. Your commute is now a pendulum.
- The 42 minutes assumes Earth has the same density all the way through. It doesn't: the core is much denser. With the real density, the trip is a bit shorter, **about 38 minutes**. That figure is from a 2015 paper by Klotz, from memory, so check it before it goes anywhere.

**What would actually happen:**
- **Air.** With air in the tunnel you'd hit terminal velocity, about 200 km/h, and stay there. You'd lose energy every time you passed the center, swing back and forth less and less, and end up stuck at the middle of the Earth.
- **The air also gets thick.** It's a column thousands of kilometers deep, so the pressure keeps climbing on the way down until the air is dense enough to crush you.
- **Heat.** The core is around 5,000°C, roughly as hot as the surface of the sun (from memory, check it). You don't come out the other side. You come out cooked.
- **Rotation.** Unless you dug pole to pole, Earth's spin moves the tunnel walls sideways relative to you (the Coriolis effect), and you scrape along the wall the whole way down.
- **The hole.** It can't exist. Rock under that much pressure flows like putty and closes the tunnel. The engineering problem comes before the physics problem.
- **Where you'd pop out.** Most land on Earth has ocean directly opposite it. Dig straight down from most of the US and you surface in the southern Indian Ocean (check this too). You'd survive the core and the pendulum and then drown.

So the honest answer: in a perfect vacuum, a 38-minute weightless ride that never ends. In reality, you get pressure-cooked at the bottom of a tube that shouldn't exist.

The fun part is that the 42 minutes doesn't depend on where you dig. Any straight tunnel between two points on the surface takes the same time if there's no friction: New York to London, or pole to pole. That's the "gravity train." Physicists have seriously proposed it more than once (from memory, check it). It's ridiculous, the math works, and the only thing stopping it is the tunnel.

### Stock Claude

Assuming the impossible parts are solved (the hole doesn't collapse and you don't instantly cook), here's what physics says, from idealized to realistic.

#### The idealized version (vacuum, non-rotating Earth, uniform density)

- **You'd feel weightless the whole way.** You're in free fall, so it feels like skydiving without wind.
- **Gravity weakens as you go down.** Only the mass *below* you pulls you, so the force shrinks linearly to zero at the center. That makes you a perfect harmonic oscillator, like a mass on a spring.
- **You'd hit about 7.9 km/s (~28,000 km/h) at the center.** That's the same speed as low Earth orbit, which is not a coincidence.
- **You'd reach the other side in about 42 minutes.** You'd arrive at exactly zero speed, pop out at the surface for an instant, then fall back. You'd oscillate forever, with a round trip of about 84 minutes.
- **Fun fact:** A straight tunnel between *any* two points on Earth (not just through the center) also takes about 42 minutes. This is called the "gravity train."

#### Real density

Earth's core is much denser than its crust, so gravity actually *increases* slightly as you descend until you near the core. This makes the trip faster, about **38 minutes**.

#### With air in the tunnel

This changes everything. You'd hit terminal velocity (~200 km/h near the surface, slower as air thickens with depth). Each pass loses energy, so you'd fall short of the far end, swing back and forth with shrinking amplitude, and **end up floating at the center of the Earth.** The air down there would also be crushingly dense from the weight of thousands of km of atmosphere above it.

#### Earth's rotation

The surface moves eastward at up to ~1,670 km/h, but points deeper down move slower. As you fall, you'd keep your eastward speed, drift ahead of the tunnel, and **scrape along the wall.** The only fix is a pole-to-pole tunnel, or a vacuum tunnel curved to match your path.

#### The actually lethal stuff

- **Heat:** The core is ~5,000–6,000 °C, about as hot as the Sun's surface.
- **Pressure:** ~3.6 million atmospheres at the center. No material could hold the tunnel open; rock flows like putty under those conditions.
- **Magma:** The outer core is liquid iron, and you'd need to tunnel through ~2,200 km of it.

**Short version:** In theory, you get a weightless 38-minute ride to the other side, then bounce back and forth forever. In practice, you get scraped against the walls, slowed by air, and incinerated somewhere in the mantle.
